\section{Introduction}
Damping modelling is a central component of dynamic analysis because it controls the decay of transient response and the amplitude of forced vibration.
In practical finite element analysis, viscous damping models are commonly used because they are simple to implement and can be incorporated directly into standard time integration schemes.
However, proportional or sparse viscous models may offer limited flexibility when a prescribed damping level must be maintained over a broad frequency range, especially in large nonlinear systems.

Nonviscous damping provides an alternative representation in which the damping force depends on the history of the response through hereditary or internal-variable descriptions.
Recent formulations have shown that such models can produce nearly uniform damping over a target frequency interval while remaining suitable for nonlinear dynamic analysis \citep{Huang2019}.
Efficient evaluation strategies based on first-order internal variables or exponential kernels further make these models attractive for large-scale computations \citep{Chang2024}.

This paper develops a unified framework for the construction of several alternative nonviscous damping formulations.
The proposed approach introduces additive energy-dissipating forces governed by first-order differential equations and examines different choices of the driving field, including displacement-, velocity- and acceleration-dependent forms associated with stiffness and mass contributions.
The resulting formulations are analysed in the frequency domain to identify their storage and loss stiffnesses, admissible pairs of complex parameters, stability requirements, and asymptotic behaviour.

Particular attention is paid to the equivalence between reciprocal formulations and to the correction terms required to recover the correct static stiffness.
These observations lead to practical formulations that preserve the size of the original dynamic system while allowing the damping contribution to be embedded into a standard direct integration workflow through a modified residual and consistent tangent effective stiffness.
Numerical examples are then used to verify convergence, demonstrate equivalence among selected formulations, and assess computational performance in a large finite element model.
