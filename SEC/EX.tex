\section{Numerical Examples}
\subsection{A SDOF System}
We consider a SDOF system with natural frequency $\omega_n$ and damping parameters $m$ and $s$, following \eqsref{eq:u_right}.
The system undergoes free vibration with initial conditions $u(0)=1$ and $\dot{u}(0)=0$.
\begin{gather}
    \left\{\begin{array}{l}
        \ddot{u}+\left(1-\dfrac{m}{s}\right)\omega_n^2u+f=0, \\
        \dot{f}=-sf+m\omega^2_nu.
    \end{array}\right.
\end{gather}
The following parameters are used in the numerical simulation: $\omega_n=10$, $m=-2$, $s=10$.
In this case, the initial condition for acceleration $a_0=-120$ needs to be properly assigned.
This value \num{-120} shall be interpreted as the original \num{-100} plus additional \num{-20} associated with $h_0$.
How this initial condition is assigned is an implementation detail and is not discussed
here.
It is not a concern in general if the global time integration is self-starting.

\figref{fig:sdof} shows the displacement history of the SDOF system with various time step sizes $\Delta{}t$ and the corresponding error convergence rate.
It is clear that second-order convergence of the used Newmark method is retained.
\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{PIC/UDD.SDOF.pdf}
    \caption{displacement history and error analysis of SDOF oscillator (free vibration)}\label{fig:sdof}
\end{figure}
\figref{fig:sdof_error} further shows the absolute error history of the SDOF system in the time domain.
\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{PIC/UDD.SDOF.ERROR.pdf}
    \caption{absolute error history of SDOF oscillator (free vibration)}\label{fig:sdof_error}
\end{figure}

One can further validate that this example can be equivalently expressed in the form of \eqsref{eq:a_right}, with $m=25/18$ and $s=25/3$.
To maintain brevity, these results are omitted since they are identical to those already presented.

In the absence of $\bh$, noting that the storage stiffness would gradually decrease as frequency approaches the left end of the approximation range.
Outside the approximation range, it can be as low as \SI{68}{\percent} of the static stiffness.
This lower bound is controlled by the summation $\sum{}m_j/s_j$, which roughly translates to the area under the curve of the specific damping factor.

It can be seen from \figref{fig:mass_eq_wider} that the storage stiffness is only \SI{14}{\percent} of the static stiffness below \num{0.001} rad/s.
At \num{1} rad/s, it is below \SI{60}{\percent} of the static stiffness.

The storage stiffness could decrease below zero under certain circumstances, for example, a moderately high damping with moderate approximation range.
Similar phenomena can also be observed in formulations \secref{sec:v_dep_k} and \secref{sec:v_dep_m}, in which hardening at high frequencies would be observed.
For structural engineering applications, a practical frequency range of interest is typically from \num{0.1} rad/s to \num{100} rad/s, which is about three orders of magnitude.
To cover this range, the target system can only be very lightly damped, otherwise the storage stiffness would deviate significantly from the static stiffness at either end of the approximation range.
